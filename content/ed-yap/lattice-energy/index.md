### What is lattice energy?
- Lattice energy is the change in enthalpy when 1 mole of an ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions.
- e.g. the lattice enthalpy for NaCl is the enthalpy change for the reaction Na<sup>+</sup> (g) + Cl<sup>-</sup> (g) -> NaCl (s)
- In order to form the gaseous ions, we must “atomize” the atom/molecule: this means to change the state of the atom/molecule to gaseous as well as break all intermolecular bonds. (when atomized, the substance is in monoatomic gaseous state)
- Standard enthalpy change of atomization is the energy change when 1 mole of gaseous **ATOMS** is formed under standard conditions.
- e.g. Li (s) -> Li (g) OR ½ Cl <sub>2</sub> (g) -> Cl (g)
- This is always endothermic, as bonds are broken and energy is supplied to change state.

### Electron Affinity
- When an atom accepts an electron, the change in enthalpy is called “electron affinity”.
- First Electron Affinity (EA1): The energy change when 1 mole of a gaseous atom gains 1 mole of electrons to form 1 mole of gaseous anion with a single negative charge under standard conditions.
- Similarly, second EA (EA2) is when 1 mole of electrons are added to 1 mole of singly charged gaseous anion to form one mole of doubly charged anions under standard conditions.
- For non-metals, EA1 is usually negative (exothermic). However, shielding and like-charge repulsion results in an endothermic EA2.

### Trends
#### Electron Affinity:
- Increases across a period: Nuclear charge and electronegativity increase and atomic radius decreases.
- Decreases down the group: Decreased electronegativity and increased atomic radius and shielding.
#### Lattice Enthalpy
- With an increased ion size, the bond formed is weaker, therefore the lattice energy decreases. True for both cation and anion.
- With a greater charge on the ion(s), the bond formed is stronger, therefore the lattice energy increases. Again, true for both ions.

### Ion Polarization:
- Sometimes, the positive charge on a cation can “distort” the electron cloud on an anion (this changes its shape).
- The power of a cation to do this is polarizing power, and this is DIRECTLY related to charge density. (therefore, polarizing power is high with small +2 or +3 cations)
- The anion’s polarizability also affects polarizing power (high with large, -2/-3 anions)
- If the bond is polarized, the ionic bond is weakened. This decreases the thermal stability.
- Therefore, thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates increases down the group, meaning the energy (heat) required to break down the compound increases.

### Enthalpy changes in solution:
- The standard enthalpy change in solution is the energy absorbed/released when 1 mole of an ionic solid dissolves in sufficient water to form an **INFINITELY** (or very) dilute solution under standard conditions.
- Negative or slightly positive values of this mean that the compound is soluble in water.
- When a compound dissolves in water, it forms ion-dipole bonds with the water molecules.
- Here, the lattice energy is reversed (lattice is broken up) and hydration energy is added (the ions form ion-dipole bonds). Therefore, the formula for ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = -ΔH<sub>latt</sub> + ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> = ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> – ΔH<sub>latt</sub>.
- Standard enthalpy change of hydration is the enthalpy change when 1 mole of a gaseous ions dissolves in sufficient water to form an infinitely dilute solution under standard conditions.
- ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> is **ALWAYS** exothermic, but more exothermic with smaller ions and larger charge (charge density)
- ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> = ΔH<sub>latt</sub> + ΔH<sub>sol</sub>
 
### The Born-Haber Cycle:
- We can state that there is a certain ΔH<sub>1</sub>, which accounts for converting Li(s) + ½F<sub>2</sub> (g) into Li<sup>+</sup>(g) and F<sup>-</sup>(g). Then, these can be converted to LiF(s), which is ΔH<sub>latt</sub> or Li(s) + ½F<sub>2</sub>(g) can be directly converted to LiF(s), which is ΔH<sub>f</sub>, the enthalpy of formation. Therefore, ΔH<sub>f</sub> = ΔH<sub>latt</sub> + ΔH<sub>1</sub>.
- This is true by Hess's Law, since we are starting with the same reactants and ending with the same products.
- This is called the Born-Haber cycle, with a diagram drawn showing all the steps to atomize each element, then combine them (the lattice energy), with the single-step process being the enthalpy of formation.

![The Born-Haber Cycle for NaCl (credit to Chemistry Learner)](img/Born-Haber-Cycle.jpg)
 
- This is an example of the Born-Haber cycle. The ΔH<sub>1</sub> value can be calculated by adding the enthalpy of atomisation, ionization energy(s), the other enthalpy of atomisation and electron affinity(s).
- To summarize: ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Cation) + ΣIEs(Cation) + ΔH<sub>at</sub>(Anion) + ΣEAs(Anion) + ΔH<sub>latt</sub>(Compound) = ΔH<sub>f</sub>(Compound) by Hess's Law.
### Solubility of Sulphates and Hydroxides
- The solubility of sulphates *DECREASES* down a group, due to the value of ΔHsol becoming more endothermic. Here’s why:
    - ΔH<sub>sol</sub> = ΔH<sub>hyd</sub> – ΔH<sub>latt</sub>. As SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup> is a large ion, it mostly contributes to the lattice energy. Therefore, the lattice energy remains relatively constant and the hydration energy keeps increasing (less negative) down the group.
    - Due to this increase, enthalpy of solubility is highest at the top of the group (most soluble) and lowest at the bottom (least soluble)
- It goes the exact opposite for hydroxides:
    - As the hydroxide ion is small, it contributes mostly to the hydration enthalpy, therefore, it stays constant, whereas the lattice enthalpy keeps decreasing (less negative) going down the group. Due to this, the most soluble compound is at the bottom and the least soluble is at the top.
